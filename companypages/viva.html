Overview

What Viva.com does

Viva.com — known for most of its history as Viva Wallet — is a European "neobank for businesses," offering card acceptance, business accounts and payment infrastructure built entirely on cloud technology (Microsoft Azure). Founded in 2000 in Athens by Haris Karonis as software house Realize SA, the company pivoted into payments with Viva Payments in 2011, obtained an Electronic Money Institution licence in 2014, and acquired Greek lender Praxia Bank in 2020 to create its own banking subsidiary, Vivabank.

In January 2022, JPMorgan Chase agreed to acquire a 48.5% stake in Viva Wallet Holdings Software Development S.A. for a reported $800 million-plus, making Viva the first Greek fintech "unicorn." The relationship later soured: JPMorgan and founder Haris Karonis filed competing legal claims in 2024 over valuation and investor rights, a dispute a London High Court ruling resolved in mid-2024 — reportedly clearing the way for a potential future sale of the company. The group's principal operating entities are Viva Wallet Holdings SA (parent), Viva Payment Services SA (the EMI and Principal Member of Visa and Mastercard), and Vivabank SA (the licensed Greek credit institution).

What it sells

Services

Viva.com's offering spans in-person and online card acceptance, business banking, and increasingly fiscalisation and back-office tools for merchants across Europe.

Card acceptance (POS)

Smart Android terminals, "Tap on Phone" (turns any NFC-enabled phone into a card reader) and "Tap to Pay on iPhone," accepting more than 30 international and local payment methods.

Online payments

Payment gateways and "Smart Checkout" for e-commerce, supporting cards, digital wallets (Apple Pay, Google Pay) and local payment methods across its European footprint.

Business accounts & cards

Business current accounts with local IBANs and a Viva business Mastercard debit card, enabled by Vivabank's Greek banking licence.

Merchant finance

Merchant cash advances and lending products, leveraging the group's own banking licence rather than routing through a third-party lender.

Fiscalisation & compliance tools

Country-specific e-invoicing and fiscal-receipt integration (e.g. for Greece, Austria, France, Germany, Italy, Portugal, Spain), addressing local tax-reporting requirements for merchants.

Viva.com Wallet

A consumer digital wallet enabling online payments in Greece, Malta and Cyprus using a personal Viva account, alongside e-banking and account-management tools for business customers.

How it earns

Revenue streams

Viva.com is fundamentally a payments acquirer: it earns from the transaction volume it processes for merchants, supplemented by banking-style lending income.

Core streams

  • Card acceptance and acquiring fees on merchant transaction volume
  • Interchange and scheme-related fees as a Principal Member of Visa and Mastercard
  • Interest income on merchant cash advances and lending, enabled by Vivabank's banking licence
  • Fees for value-added services such as fiscalisation, expense management and gift-card/loyalty tools

Pricing model

  • Viva markets a 0% commission proposition for merchants who combine its card-acceptance service with use of the Viva business debit card for expenses
  • Standard acquiring fees apply outside that bundled model, positioned to compete with acquirers such as SumUp, Dojo, Flatpay and myPOS

Reported figures vary significantly by source and year: Viva Wallet Group's own 2021 annual report showed group revenues of €82 million (more than double 2020), while a UK subsidiary's 2023 accounts reportedly showed a £46 million operating loss on £57 million revenue — a reminder that country-level and group-level figures are not the same measure. No single, up-to-date, audited consolidated revenue figure for the full Viva.com Group was identified in the sources reviewed for this profile.

Where it operates

Markets

Viva.com's stated strategy is deliberately "local": rather than relying purely on EU passporting from Greece, it has built physical, regulatory branches in most of the countries it serves.

Corporate & regulatory footprint

  • Greece (Athens HQ) — home market; Vivabank is a Bank of Greece-supervised credit institution
  • Physical branches or offices across 23-29 European countries, including Malta, Cyprus, the UK, Belgium, Romania, Slovenia, Slovakia and Latvia
  • A UK subsidiary, Viva Wallet.com Ltd, files its own annual accounts

Focus & positioning

  • Primarily small and medium-sized businesses, alongside larger retail chains and enterprise merchants
  • Competes in the fast-growing European "tap-to-pay" acquirer segment alongside SumUp, Dojo, Flatpay and myPOS
  • Non-Greek European volume has exceeded 50% of total processed volume since as early as 2021, reflecting the depth of its pan-European push

Regional relationship

Relationship with Malta

Malta is one of a small number of markets Viva.com treats as a core, named territory for its consumer wallet product — not simply one of many countries reached through passporting.

A named core market with a local office

  • Viva.com maintains a registered office in Malta (Gzira), with a dedicated local support telephone line — a level of physical presence beyond simple cross-border service provision.
  • Viva.com Wallet, the company's consumer-facing digital wallet product, is explicitly scoped to "merchants in Greece, Malta, and Cyprus" — placing Malta alongside Viva's home market of Greece as one of only three countries where this specific product operates.
  • Malta account holders go through Viva's standard KYC/business-establishment verification process, consistent with its treatment as a fully served, compliance-covered market rather than a passporting afterthought.

This is a narrower but more concentrated relationship than some other companies in this index: rather than holding an MFSA licence of its own, Viva.com appears to serve Malta through its Greek EMI/banking licence passported under PSD2 — but the operational commitment (a local office, a support line, and Malta's inclusion as a named market for a specific consumer product) suggests Malta is treated as a priority market rather than an incidental one.
